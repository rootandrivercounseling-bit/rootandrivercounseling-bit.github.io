The word soma comes from the Greek word for body. A somatic approach recognizes that our emotional experiences aren't only thoughts; they can also be experienced through the body.

You may have noticed this yourself. Anxiety might show up as a tight chest or difficulty taking a full breath. Stress may create tension in your neck and shoulders. Shame might feel like heat in your face or pressure behind your eyes. You may notice your stomach tighten when you're overwhelmed, or feel numb, heavy, or disconnected when you've experienced too much for too long.

Our bodies respond to what we experience. Trauma, chronic stress, rejection, shame, and major life transitions can all influence how we experience ourselves physically and emotionally. Sometimes we learn to push difficult feelings away, stay busy, minimize what happened, or simply keep moving forward. These strategies can help us function, but they don't necessarily resolve what we're carrying.

Somatic therapy offers another way of working

Rather than immediately analyzing or talking through every detail of your experience, we can slow down and become curious about what is happening in your body in the present moment. We might notice tension, constriction, numbness, activation, or other sensations and learn to approach them gradually and safely.

The goal isn't to force a feeling to disappear. It's to develop a different relationship with your internal experience: to recognize what your body may be communicating and build greater capacity to experience difficult emotions without becoming overwhelmed by them.

Internal Family Systems (IFS)

I also incorporate Internal Family Systems (IFS) into my work. IFS is based on the idea that our minds naturally contain different “parts” that can have different emotions, needs, beliefs, and protective roles.

You might recognize a part of yourself that is highly critical, another that constantly worries, another that wants to avoid difficult situations, or another that feels deeply hurt or rejected.

Rather than judging or trying to eliminate these parts, IFS invites us to become curious about them.

What is this part trying to protect me from?

What does it need?

When did it learn that it needed to take on this role?

By developing a more compassionate relationship with these different parts of ourselves, we can begin to understand patterns that may have once felt confusing or overwhelming. IFS and somatic therapy can work particularly well together because we can explore both what a part is experiencing emotionally and how that experience is showing up in the body.

Therapy becomes less about fighting against yourself and more about developing a deeper understanding of yourself.

At Root & River Counseling in Boise, I use somatic therapy and IFS alongside other approaches to help clients experiencing trauma, anxiety, emotional overwhelm, shame, relationship difficulties, and chronic stress develop greater self-awareness, regulation, and connection.
